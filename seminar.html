Why networking is a security skill

Every attack that involves communication depends on a network. If you cannot trace how data moves, you cannot understand how it is intercepted — or where to defend it.

Remember

Network = local group of devices. Internet = network of networks. Data travels a step-by-step journey, and every hop is an exposure point.

TIME00:05 – 00:09 · 4 minutes · SM1.0 serials 1.1 and 1.2

WHAT TO EXPLAINRead the definition, then walk the flow left to right. The teaching point: the message is not sent as one piece. It is broken into data packets, sent through a chain of devices, and reassembled at the destination. Then make the security turn: the same chain that makes communication possible makes interception possible.

EXAMPLEThe course's own example: sending an email from a café's free Wi-Fi. Your laptop does not send directly to your supervisor — the router forwards it to the ISP, then across many systems, then to the destination server. You control neither the router nor the path.

03 · NETWORKING · SM1.0 — 1.4, 1.7

Types of network, and how they are arranged

00:14 – 00:19

Kinds of network — by size and coverage

PANAround one person — a few metres
LANLocal — home, office, classroom, shop
CANCampus — several buildings on one site
MANMetropolitan — a city or large institution
WANWide — cities, countries, continents

Also classified by purpose: EPN (electronic payment), SAN (storage), VPN (virtual private).

Topology — the arrangement of those connections

Bus — cheap; one break stops everything. Shared path makes monitoring easy.

Star — the common default. If the central switch fails, the whole network is down.

Mesh — many paths, very resilient; more connections mean a larger attack surface.

Remember

Topology decides what happens when something fails — and the security question it answers is: if an attacker gets in, how far can they go?

There is no perfect topology

The best choice depends on budget, performance, security requirements and business need. A well-designed topology limits damage; a poor one makes attacks easier.

TIME00:14 – 00:19 · 5 minutes · SM1.0 serials 1.4 and 1.7

WHAT TO EXPLAINTwo ideas. First, networks are classified by size and coverage — PAN up to WAN — and the bigger and more distributed it is, the less any single party controls it. Second, topology is the arrangement, and it determines resilience and exposure.

EXAMPLEThe course's topology competition: a company with 100 employees asks "how will you connect everything together?" — before choosing a single router or switch. Draw out that star is today's default, and that a bank might reasonably choose mesh for availability.

EXAM FOCUSExpect "which topology has a single point of failure?" (star → the central switch; bus → the backbone) and "which LAN type covers a campus?" (CAN). Keep the answers to one line.

04 · NETWORKING · SM2.0 — 2.1, 2.2

Protocols: the rules that make communication possible

00:19 – 00:24
CORE

A protocol is a standardised set of rules governing how devices communicate. Different devices, different operating systems, different makers — they only work together because they follow the same rules.

What a protocol defines

01How to start communication
02How to send data
03How to receive data
04How to detect errors
05How to end communication

Security connection

Every secure connection, encrypted website and authentication system depends on protocols. If a protocol is poorly designed or misconfigured, attackers may exploit it.

Protocol categories

CommunicationTCP · UDP · IP — device-to-device delivery
WebHTTP · HTTPS — browser asks, server answers
EmailSMTP sends · POP3 downloads · IMAP manages on the server

Remember — TCP · UDP · IP

TCP delivers reliably and in order, and retransmits what is lost. UDP sends as fast as possible and does not care about loss. IP is the address that gets data to the right device.

TIME00:19 – 00:24 · 5 minutes · SM2.0 serials 2.1 and 2.2

WHAT TO EXPLAINAnchor on the course's own example: a group of friends on Android, iPhone, macOS and Windows all joining the same game lobby. That only works because every device follows the same rules. Then list the five things a protocol defines — it is a clean, memorable list.

EXAMPLEFor TCP vs UDP at this level, use the course's two examples: printing a document (every page must arrive, so TCP retransmits) versus a live broadcast that lags for five seconds (UDP skips the lost seconds and continues with the latest data).

EXAM FOCUSKnow the categories and which protocol sits in which. Know that SMTP sends, POP3 downloads, IMAP keeps mail on the server. HTTPS is HTTP with encryption in transit.

Important — what OSI is for

The OSI model is not directly implemented. Real systems run TCP/IP. OSI is a conceptual framework, and it is used for troubleshooting: if a website will not load, work down the layers to find where it breaks.

Two layers worth naming separately

Presentation (L6) — different devices support different formats. This layer converts between them, and it is where encryption, decryption and compression happen.

Session (L5) — before communication can succeed, a conversation must be established and managed. Think of joining an online class: session created, suspended if you leave, re-established if you rejoin, terminated when the meeting ends.

Remember

Seven layers, top to bottom: Application · Presentation · Session · Transport · Network · Data Link · Physical. Trouble at the bottom (cable) looks nothing like trouble at the top (application).

TIME00:24 – 00:30 · 6 minutes · SM2.0 serial 2.4

WHAT TO EXPLAINGive the order first, out loud, twice. Then explain why the model exists — troubleshooting. The course's own example: a website is not accessible; is it the cable, the IP address, the application, or the server? Without a structured model you are guessing.

EXAMPLEUse the two the course uses. Presentation: one computer sends Unicode, another uses ASCII — this layer converts. Compression: sending a picture on a messaging app compresses before sending to use less bandwidth. Session: an online class room — join, leave, rejoin, meeting ends.

EXAM FOCUSLayer order is the single most likely OSI question. Then: "which layer does encryption?" (Presentation), "which layer uses MAC addresses?" (Data Link), "which layer makes frames?" (Data Link).

Why layers at all

If every protocol tried to do everything, communication would be unmanageable. Each layer has one job, so a problem or an attack can be located precisely.

Remember

09 · NETWORKING · SM4.0 — 4.1, 4.2

Basic network attacks

00:49 – 00:55
CORE

A network attack is an attempt to intercept, manipulate, impersonate or disrupt communication between devices on a network. Every one of those maps onto a leg of the CIA triad.

Man-in-the-Middle

The attacker secretly positions themselves between two parties who believe they are talking directly to each other.

Real example: public Wi-Fi attacks, rogue access points, session hijacking.

Denial of Service

Overwhelming a service with traffic or requests so legitimate users can no longer use it.

Targets availability: website downtime, resource exhaustion, financial loss.

Spoofing

Pretending to be a trusted person, organisation, device or system.

Email spoofing, IP spoofing, website spoofing, caller ID spoofing.

Man-in-the-Browser

Malware inside the browser secretly intercepts and manipulates data in real time.

Targets the web session: transaction manipulation, credential theft.

A firewall is one layer only

It filters traffic. It cannot repair insecure application logic, and it cannot stop an authorised user misusing their own access. The course shows exactly this: a rule blocking external grade changes is bypassed by manipulating request headers.

Segmentation — dividing the network into zones

Splitting a network by usage, function or organisational requirement, typically enforced with firewall rules.

Remember

Segmentation reduces the attack surface, contains damage to one zone, improves monitoring, and enforces least privilege. An attacker who reaches one zone cannot simply walk into the others.

EXAM CHECK Press A / B / C / D — then ENTER to reveal

Which statement about network segmentation is correct?

B. Segmentation divides the network into zones so a compromised host stays contained. It reduces the attack surface, and it is often enforced by firewall rules — so it certainly does not replace the firewall or authentication.

11 · CYBERSECURITY · SM1.0 — 1.1 to 1.4

What cybersecurity actually protects

01:00 – 01:06
CORE

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting the systems, networks and data an organisation depends on — keeping them safe from anyone who should not be able to see, change, or disrupt them.

Protecting confidentiality

Keeping information away from anyone not authorised to see it. A competitor should not read the shipment contracts.

Protecting integrity

Making sure information stays accurate and unaltered. A quietly changed delivery address sends the truck to the wrong place.

Protecting availability

Making sure systems and data are there when needed. A warehouse system that is offline is useless even if its records are correct.

The five scopes — every layer must hold

NetworkProtects the pathways data travels — firewalls, routing, monitoring
ApplicationProtects the software itself, built into the code from the start
InformationProtects the data wherever it lives — server, spreadsheet, laptop
CloudProtects data on infrastructure you do not physically own
EndpointProtects the individual devices — laptops, phones, scanners

Remember — a practice, not a product

Cybersecurity is not software you install once. It is a continuous cycle of understanding risk, reducing it, watching for signs, and responding. Buying security is not the same as practising it.

Why "we are too small to be a target" is a myth

Automated attacks are indiscriminate. Smaller organisations often have fewer defences, and valuable access into larger partners' systems — so they become a stepping stone, not an invisible target.

TIME01:00 – 01:06 · 6 minutes · Cybersecurity SM1.0 serials 1.1–1.4

WHAT TO EXPLAINReset after the break. Give the definition, then the three harms — because every technical term for the rest of the hour is a more specific version of one of them. Then the five scopes, and the point that no single scope covers for the others.

EXAMPLEThe incident log from the course: every row fell into one of the three buckets. A guessed vendor login (confidentiality), an invoice total edited and unnoticed for a week (integrity), an ordering system down six hours in a storm (availability — and nothing was "hacked" at all).

Remember — it is a balance, not a checklist

The three goals are in tension. Strengthening one can damage another. Encrypting everything so heavily that staff cannot work damages availability — and drives people to unsafe workarounds, damaging confidentiality too.

The classic trap

A password policy demanding a new long password every 30 days maximises confidentiality on paper — until people start writing passwords on sticky notes. A well-intentioned control can undermine the very goal it was meant to serve.

A system is configured so strictly that legitimate staff can no longer do their jobs. Which leg of the CIA triad is most directly damaged?

C — Availability. A control that stops legitimate users reaching data damages availability, even though nothing was stolen and nothing was altered. Authentication is not a leg of the triad at all — it is a separate process.

01:13 – 01:21
CORE COMPARE

Three different things that only become dangerous when they line up together. Use one picture for all three: an old fence with a loose plank that does not latch.

Vulnerability — the loose plank

A weakness

A weakness in a system, process or piece of software that could be exploited. It exists whether or not anyone ever tries.

Unpatched software · a misconfigured router · a door that does not latch

Threat — the dog

Something that can exploit it

Anything capable of exploiting a vulnerability to cause harm — a person, malicious software, or even a natural event.

An attacker · malware · a flood that damages a server room

Risk — how likely, how bad

Likelihood × impact

The realistic chance that a threat exploits a vulnerability, combined with the severity of the result. This is what teams actually prioritise on.

C — Vulnerability. It exists independently of any attacker. A threat is what could exploit it; risk is the likelihood and impact of that actually happening.

WHAT TO EXPLAINTell the fence story in full before showing any definition. Vulnerability = the loose plank. Threat = the dog. Risk = will that dog find that plank, and how bad is the result. Then read the three cards — the definitions will feel obvious because the picture is already in their heads.

EXAMPLEThe course's low-stakes example: an old internal tool with a known, unpatched vulnerability — but reachable only from inside the building, with no external access. The vulnerability is real; the realistic threat that can reach it is almost absent; so the risk is low. That is the lesson: we do not panic about every vulnerability equally.

EXAM FOCUSThis is the most likely source of a scenario question — give them a situation and ask them to label it. Say the sentence aloud: a weakness, something that can use it, and how likely and how bad the combination is.

TRANSITION"So how do we handle risk? Three options — reduce, transfer, accept. Then: what are the threats themselves?"

14 · CYBERSECURITY · SM3.0 — 3.1, 3.3, 3.4

Common cyber threats

01:21 – 01:30
Malware type
Basic idea — and how it behaves
Virus
Attaches to a legitimate file and spreads when that file is shared or run. Needs a host and a trigger.
Worm
Spreads on its own across a network, with no human action at all. Faster than a virus.
Trojan
Disguised as something legitimate or desirable, so the user runs it willingly. Relies entirely on the disguise.
Ransomware
Encrypts the victim's files and demands payment for the decryption key.
Categories are not exclusive. Real incidents combine them: a trojan delivers, then ransomware encrypts — one attack chain, two labels.

Password attacks

Brute forceTry every possible combination — slow against a long, random password
DictionaryTry common words and previously leaked passwords — much faster
Credential stuffingReuse logins leaked from one breach against other sites — nothing is guessed
Phishing for loginsSimply ask, through a fake login page

Remember — why reuse is the real danger

Credential stuffing needs no guessing at all. A password's strength matters far less if it has been reused somewhere that later gets breached.

Malicious insider
Negligent insider
Compromised insider
Deliberately misuses legitimate access — stealing, sabotage, leaking
Causes harm through carelessness — misconfiguration, lost device, falling for phishing
A legitimate account taken over by an outside attacker; activity looks authorised

The defence that works for all three — least privilege

Limit what any one account can reach. Then whether the insider is malicious, careless or compromised, the damage is contained. Limiting access beats judging character.

EXAMPLEThe course's incident: a shipping-confirmation attachment that was really a trojan, which then downloaded ransomware. Ask the room whether it was a trojan or ransomware — the answer is both, at different stages. That is the attack-chain idea.

15 · CYBERSECURITY · SM3.0 — 3.2 · SM5.0 — 5.1

Phishing and social engineering

01:30 – 01:38

Read this message — find what is wrong before I say it

FromShipping Carrier <support@shipping-carrler.com>
ToDear Customer
SubjectURGENT: your parcel will be returned in 2 hours

Your delivery could not be completed. Immediate action is required or the parcel will be returned to sender.

Confirm your details here to reschedule: http://shipping-carrler.com/track/verify

📎 Attachment: Delivery_Confirmation_Invoice.zip

— The Shipping Carrier Team

The five red flags

1Sender address, not the display name. "shipping-carrler" has an extra letter — a single swapped character. Read the actual address, never the name.
2Manufactured urgency. "Immediate action required", "2 hours" — pressure is designed to stop you reading carefully.
3The link does not match. Hover, do not click. The true destination is frequently not what the text claims.
4Generic greeting on a personal message. "Dear Customer" from someone claiming an urgent personal matter is a mismatch.
5Unexpected attachment. An invoice or archive you were not expecting is the classic delivery mechanism for malware.

And the techniques behind it

Phishing — bulk fake messages. Spear phishing — researched and targeted, far harder to spot. Pretexting — a fabricated scenario, e.g. "I'm from IT, I need your password". Baiting — something enticing, like a USB drive left in a car park.

SCENARIO

A colleague forwards you this email and asks what you think. What should you suspect — and what should they do?

"Your account will be deleted in 10 minutes. Click this link immediately to keep access."

PHISHING · SOCIAL ENGINEERING

Two of the red flags are present at once: manufactured urgency ("in 10 minutes") and a link to click. There is no technical flaw being exploited here — the attack targets judgment, which is why the correct response is procedural, not technical: do not click, do not reply, and report it to the security team. The pause is the control.

TIME01:30 – 01:38 · 8 minutes · Cybersecurity SM3.0 serial 3.2, practical SM5.0 serial 5.1

WHAT TO EXPLAINDo not reveal anything. Show the message and ask the room to find what is wrong — that is exactly the 5.1 exercise. Then walk the five flags using the colour coding on the message. Finish with the techniques, making sure they can separate phishing (the broad category) from social engineering (the umbrella — manipulating people, not machines).

EXAMPLEThe course's own detail: the domain was "shipping-carrler.com" with a single swapped letter, while the formatting, logo and tone were a near-perfect copy. And the honest note — the student in the lab missed a well-crafted spear-phishing email precisely because it used no urgency language at all. Urgency is common, not required.

EXAM FOCUSName at least three red flags; distinguish phishing from spear phishing; and know that urgency is the most common lever. Then run the scenario and reveal.

TRANSITION"Phishing steals credentials. So what should protect them?"

MFA = requiring more than one family together, so a stolen password alone is no longer enough. Authenticator app and hardware key are the strongest; SMS codes are weakest (SIM swapping).

Remember — the order matters

Authentication must come first. An authorization decision is meaningless until identity is confirmed. A badge that correctly identifies someone but opens the wrong door is valid authentication and failed authorization.

MFA is not unbreakable — MFA fatigue

Attackers send repeated approval prompts, hoping a tired user taps approve to stop the notifications. An unexpected approval request deserves the same pause as a suspicious email — deny it and report it.

Encryption basics — where it fits

Encryption turns readable plaintext into unreadable ciphertext, reversible only with the key. It protects confidentiality — not availability, not integrity — and a stolen key defeats it entirely.

EXAM CHECK Press A / B / C / D — then ENTER to reveal

A badge correctly identifies an employee but lets them into a room they should not enter. What has failed?

B — Authorization. The badge proved identity, so authentication worked perfectly. The failure is in what that confirmed identity is permitted to reach — a separate system, configured separately.

TIME01:38 – 01:44 · 6 minutes · Cybersecurity SM2.0 serial 2.2, SM4.0 serial 4.2

WHAT TO EXPLAINUse the two questions as the whole slide: WHO ARE YOU, then WHAT MAY YOU DO. Say the order rule out loud — it is the examinable point. Then the three factor families, then MFA as "more than one family at once".

EXAMPLEThe badge story from the course: a former employee's badge was never deactivated. Authentication still worked perfectly — the badge really did prove who it belonged to — but nobody had updated what that identity could reach. Two separate jobs, and a company can be good at one and sloppy at the other.

EXAM FOCUSThis distinction is heavily examinable. Also know: encryption protects confidentiality, not availability or integrity; the key matters as much as the algorithm; a padlock means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is legitimate.

17 · CYBERSECURITY · SM4.0 — 4.1, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

Personal and organisational practices

01:44 – 01:50

Strong passwords

  • Length over complexity — a passphrase of unrelated words beats a short symbol mix
  • Unique everywhere — so one breach cannot reach your other accounts
  • Use a password manager; avoid predictable patterns
  • Forced monthly rotation often backfires — people make predictable tweaks

Safe browsing

  • Check for HTTPS — the padlock means the connection is encrypted
  • But it does not mean the site is trustworthy — verify the actual domain character by character
  • Download only from official sources; a convincing download button is a common trojan route
  • Treat browser pop-up urgency exactly like email urgency

Public Wi-Fi

  • Use a VPN — it encrypts traffic before it leaves your device
  • Confirm the exact network name with staff — fake hotspots imitate real ones
  • Turn off auto-connect for public networks
  • Save banking and confidential work for a trusted network
  • A VPN protects the pipe, not your judgment

Incident response

1Identify — notice something is wrong
2Contain — isolate the system
3Eradicate — remove the cause
4Recover — restore safely
5Lessons learned — review

Remember — the padlock is necessary, not sufficient

Obtaining a valid HTTPS certificate is fast and free, including for attackers running fake login pages. The padlock confirms encryption. It says nothing about who you are talking to.

Remember — containment speed decides the outcome

The biggest factor in how much damage an incident causes is how fast it is contained — not how perfectly the later steps are executed. So: notice, and report immediately. Do not investigate first, and do not wait to see if it resolves itself.

TIME01:44 – 01:50 · 6 minutes · Cybersecurity SM4.0 serials 4.1, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

WHAT TO EXPLAINFour practices, one card each. This is the "turn knowledge into habits" module. Do not linger — the two Remember boxes carry the examinable weight: HTTPS ≠ trustworthy, and containment speed beats perfection.

EXAMPLETwo from the course. First: two login pages side by side, both convincing forgeries — one with a padlock, one without — and the padlock told the student nothing about which was real. Second: the malware drill, where the instinct was to investigate the strange pop-up; the correct action was to disconnect first and ask questions afterwards.

EXAM FOCUSLearn the five incident response stages in order — that is a near-certain question. Also: what does a VPN protect against? Packet sniffing and interception of traffic — not phishing links or weak passwords.
